Calculating Statewide Election Results by Congressional and Legislative District: Statement of Methodology


The Downballot


(First published March 2021; last updated April 2025)


Starting in 2008, The Downballot[1] has calculated the results of every U.S. presidential election for each of the nation's 435 congressional districts. We have since expanded this effort to include data from additional statewide elections, such as for Senate or governor, and to cover state legislative districts as well.


This ongoing project presents a number of choices and difficulties. To further our commitment to total transparency, this statement of methodology describes our complete approach to calculating statewide election results by congressional and legislative district on a state-by-state basis. Anyone wishing to should be able to use this document to fully replicate our work.


Calculations for the 2024 election are available here. Calculations for elections from 2012 to 2020 are available here. Calculations for the 2008 election are available here.


1. Definitions


In this section, we define a number of terms that we use regularly in describing our procedures for calculating statewide election results by congressional and/or legislative district.


County: A geographic subdivision of a state that typically possesses a level of governmental authority, including the power to conduct elections. In almost every state, statewide elections are administered by county-level authorities or their equivalents.[2] Election results below the statewide level are also most commonly reported at the county level.


We consider the following subdivisions to be "county equivalents":
* boroughs and census areas in Alaska
* parishes in Louisiana
* Baltimore City in Maryland
* St. Louis City in Missouri
* Carson City in Nevada
* the 38 independent cities in Virginia
* the election authority in Chicago, Illinois, which is distinct from the election authority in the rest of Cook County[3]
* the election authority in Detroit, Michigan, which is distinct from the election authority in the rest of Wayne County
* the election authority in Kansas City, Missouri, which is distinct from the election authority in the rest of Jackson County


In addition, in Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, and Vermont, elections are administered at the city and town level instead of at the county level.


This document therefore uses the term "county" to refer to counties; county equivalents; and cities and towns in the six New England states mentioned above, unless otherwise specified.


District: A congressional or state legislative district.


Unsplit County: A county that is located entirely in one district.


Split County: A county that is divided between two or more districts.


Precinct: The smallest geographic unit used to administer elections and, generally, to report election results. Almost all states use precincts and report election results at this level.[4]


Different jurisdictions use different names for this concept. For example, precincts are referred to as "election districts" in New York; "divisions" in certain parts of Pennsylvania; and "wards" in Wisconsin. We use the term "precinct" for all such units.


Because of their granular nature, precinct results allow us to determine how different parts of the same county voted. For instance, Arizona's 5th, 6th, 7th, 8th, and 9th Congressional Districts are located entirely in Maricopa County, which voted for Joe Biden over Donald Trump by a 50-48 margin in 2020. If we were to assign the entire county's votes proportionally, that would incorrectly make it appear that Biden narrowly won each of these five districts. However, by using precinct-level results, we can determine that these districts in fact voted very differently from one another.[5]


As a consequence, we require precinct-level election results from any split counties in order to calculate its results by district. For unsplit counties, we need only county-level election results.


Split Precinct: A precinct that is divided between two or more districts. "Sub-precincts" refer to each sub-part of a split precinct. We explain further in our "Split precincts" section below.


Unassigned Votes: Votes that are reported without regard to geography below the county level.


While many election authorities assign all votes to a precinct, others report many votes (often but not exclusively absentee or mail votes) in one bloc without regard to geography.


We refer to election precincts that encompass a defined geographic area as "standard precincts," while we refer to the votes that are not assigned to a standard precinct as "unassigned votes." Unless otherwise qualified, the term "precinct" refers to standard precincts. We explain further in our "Unassigned votes" section below.


Limited Ballots: Ballots that include only certain races and do not contain a full slate of contests. For example, ballots cast under the auspices of the Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voting Act ("UOCAVA") are often limited to federal races only. Votes cast via limited ballots often result in unassigned votes.


Total Ballots Cast: The total number of votes cast in a given precinct, county, or district. These consist of the number of votes counted for each candidate listed on the ballot; all write-in votes tabulated by election authorities; undervotes (i.e., when a voter declines to cast a vote in a particular race or races on the ballot); and overvotes (i.e., when a voter attempts to select more candidates than are permitted, resulting in no votes being tallied in any such situations).


2. Data


1. Data sources


State-level election authorities in all states (with the exception of Alaska)[6] publish county-level results for every county within the state.


Precinct-level results are handled differently depending on the state. Some state-level election authorities provide precinct-level results for the entire state soon after Election Day. Other states do not release this data until well after Election Day. Still others never provide this data.


If precinct-level data is not available from state-level election authorities when we calculate the results by district, we seek this data from county-level election authorities. Much of this information is posted on county election authority websites, but sometimes, we need to contact counties for results. We explain more about this process here.


We also rely on OpenElections, a project managed by journalist Derek Willis that gathers precinct-level election results and transforms them into a consistent, machine-readable format.


We provide the source for all election results we use to calculate district-level results in the section "State-by-state Sources, Procedures, and Notes" below.


2. Official vs. unofficial results


States and counties are not required to certify their final election results for days or weeks after Election Day. Once results are certified, we refer to them as "official results." Until that process is complete, any results are unofficial, meaning that the totals are not final and more votes may be tabulated. These unofficial results can also contain errors that may be corrected during the certification process.


Because official results will often differ—sometimes materially so—from unofficial results, we only use official results for our calculations. Sometimes, election authorities will misleadingly label results as "unofficial" even though they are official; we use these if we have confirmed with the relevant authorities that these results are in fact the official totals.


Note that even official results can sometimes change after certification. When we become aware of such changes, we update our calculations accordingly and publicize any such updates.


3. Differing vote tallies between state and county authorities


In states where results are not centralized, the county election authorities' vote tallies will sometimes differ from the countywide totals provided by the state even though both results are listed as official.


We rely on the county's totals over the state's unless we have good reason to believe that the county's totals are wrong. In the past, these reasons have included situations where the county's totals have an unreasonably large or small number of votes, or tabulation errors we have discovered by looking at the county's precinct data.


4. Write-in votes


Many election authorities within the same state treat write-in votes differently. For instance, some counties will include them in their official precinct-level results, while others will treat write-in votes as unofficial and leave them out. 


To deal with these votes consistently, we do not include write-ins except in rare elections where such votes make up a material fraction of the total vote.


3. How we calculate statewide election results by district


We begin by assigning all votes from unsplit counties to their appropriate district.


We then determine which precincts in each split county belong in which district and assign those votes accordingly. We discuss this process below in the section "Assignment of precincts to districts in split counties."


We then use a variety of methods to allocate unassigned votes and votes in split precincts. We discuss this process below in the sections "Unassigned votes" and "Split precincts." We specify which methods we use for each of these tasks on a state-by-state basis in the section "State-by-state Sources, Procedures, and Notes" below.


In a few cases, local election authorities will provide the results of statewide elections broken down by district, which we will use when necessary. In 2020, for instance, to calculate presidential results by congressional district, we used such information from certain counties in California, Kentucky, Maine, and Missouri; certain municipalities in New Jersey; and the city of Chicago.


Note: In rare cases, the necessary data to perform the calculations described above is not available, making it impossible to calculate statewide election results by district.


States that release results by district


The vast majority of state election authorities do not calculate and release the results of statewide elections by district, which is why we launched this project in 2008. A handful, however, do. We will rely on such data if it includes the number of votes each candidate received in every county or split county that makes up each district. If not, our strong preference is to calculate the results ourselves using the methods described in this document.


For instance, the California Secretary of State's office publishes a Supplement to the Statement of Vote each election that provides district-level results for statewide races with our preferred level of detail at the county level, such as this example from 2016. The Texas Legislative Council, by contrast, provides district-level results for statewide races, such as this example from 2018, but does not provide county-level breakdowns.


In 2020, Nebraska was the only state whose calculations of the presidential election results by congressional district we used.


4. Assignment of precincts to districts in split counties
We most commonly determine which precincts in split counties are located in which districts by examining the results from district-level elections that take place concurrently with the statewide election we are analyzing.


For instance, if a county is split between two congressional districts, presidential results will typically be reported at the precinct level without any indication of which congressional district each precinct is located in. However, results for the U.S. House elections in that county will of necessity indicate which congressional district each precinct is located in. We use this information to assign precincts accordingly.


This information is not always available. In certain election years, no congressional districts are on the ballot at the same time as certain statewide races. For instance, Virginia holds gubernatorial elections in odd-numbered years following each presidential election.


In other elections, no or only some state legislative districts in a given chamber are on the ballot at the same time as certain statewide races. For example, elections for the Kansas Senate only take place in presidential years, not in midterms. In other states, such as California and Texas, only half of all state Senate districts are on the ballot in midterm years, while the remainder are on the ballot in presidential cycles.


Additionally, some states, such as Florida and Louisiana, do not list races on the general election ballot if only one candidate is officially competing.[7] If a county has just one race that was not on the ballot, we can use the precinct results from the others to assign the precincts to all districts. If, however, a county has two or more district races that were not on the ballot, we can use precinct results from past years only if we have verified that precinct boundaries have not changed in the interim.


However, election authorities often change precinct boundaries between elections. They may even be redrawn between the primary and general elections in the same year. Such changes may not be obvious without information from local election authorities or precinct maps. For instance, even if a county has the same number of precincts in 2020 as it did in 2018, Precinct 1 may not feature the same geographic boundaries as it did two years earlier.


Changes like these mean that we cannot reuse our assignment of precincts to districts from past years unless we can confirm that a given county's precinct boundaries have not changed during the timeframe in question. If any changes have been made to precinct boundaries, it is necessary to assign precincts to districts anew.


If we cannot verify that precinct boundaries have not changed, we contact election authorities for a list of 2020 precincts assigned by district. In rare cases where this information cannot be obtained, we must find a 2020 precinct map and visually match precincts to a district. In exceedingly rare cases, such information is not available, making it impossible to calculate district-level results for statewide races.


5. Unassigned votes


Unassigned votes are usually (though not always) votes that were cast before Election Day, such as by absentee or mail ballot, or through early voting. Other examples include provisional votes and situations where a county's official precinct-level results do not add up to that county's total number of votes for a candidate.


Such unassigned votes must be assigned to a district. We describe three different methods for allocating such votes below:


* The Assigned Votes method
* The Total Ballot method
* The Sub-County method

2. Total Ballot ("TB") Method[11]


The coronavirus pandemic led to a major expansion in the number of votes cast before Election Day in 2020, and in many states, that meant a much larger than usual proportion were not assigned to a standard precinct than ever before. We therefore devised the Total Ballot method to allocate unassigned votes to a district. The TB method can be understood as a variant or refinement of the AV method. While we consider both methods to be accurate, the TB method is more so.


We are only able to use the TB method rather than the AV method if we know how many total votes (including write-ins, overvotes, and undervotes) were cast in each precinct or district. This includes votes cast by all methods, including in-person early votes, absentee or mail votes, and in-person Election Day votes. Election authorities do not, however, always provide the total vote.


We start by assuming that how a candidate's supporters choose to cast their ballots is similar no matter where they live. For example, if 30% of Biden voters choose to vote absentee in District A, we assume somewhere around 30% of Biden voters will also choose to vote absentee in District B. (We've validated this assumption by testing it in other states that make more detailed vote tabulations available.) If this is true, then the distribution of votes for a candidate between districts will also be about the same for Election Day votes and absentee votes.

3. Sub-County ("SC") Method[13]


The Sub-County method is another variant of the AV method that, like the TB method, offers greater precision but requires the availability of particular data. Sometimes, an election authority will assign some votes to a congressional district or municipality within a county, but not to precincts. We refer to these as "sub-county" votes, and they are required to make use of the SC method.


For instance, Virginia specifies which congressional district any early votes or provisional votes were cast in, but it does not reveal which precinct or legislative district these votes were cast in. Almost all in-person votes cast on Election Day are, however, assigned to precincts.


To allocate these sub-county votes, we take into account what proportion of each congressional district overlaps with the legislative district in question using the precinct vote. To do this, we assign each precinct to both a legislative district and a congressional district.

6. Split precincts


Many states split precincts between two or more districts. In such instances, for races that take place at the district level, election officials will report the results broken down by each precinct sub-part (or "sub-precinct"). However, they typically do not report the results of statewide elections by sub-precinct.


An example follows. Below are the results of the 2016 Ohio state House races that took place in Cuyahoga County's precinct 0106 CLEVELAND-01-B (found here), with the precinct in question highlighted. This precinct was split between State House District 9 ("HD-09") and State House District 12 ("HD-12"). Clicking each image below links to a larger version.

Only a single set of presidential results is reported for precinct 0106 CLEVELAND-01-B. To know how HD-09 and HD-12 each voted on the presidential level, we must therefore allocate the presidential results from this precinct to its two sub-precincts, one in each House district.


Ideally, we would allocate the votes for each split precinct based on how many votes were cast in each sub-precinct in each legislative race. For instance, if a hypothetical Precinct A were split between two legislative districts, and 100 votes were cast for the legislative race in Sub-precinct 1 while 50 votes were cast for the legislative race in Sub-precinct 2, we would assign two-thirds of the presidential vote to Sub-precinct 1 and one-third to Sub-precinct 2.


However, because legislative or congressional races are often uncontested or minimally contested, we often see different rates of undervoting (i.e., voters skipping a race) in each sub-precinct when a precinct is split across two or more districts. In the pair of Ohio state House elections shown above, the race for HD-09 was contested between a Democratic candidate and a Republican candidate, while the race for HD-12 was not (only a Democratic candidate appeared on the ballot). 


The summary information provided by Cuyahoga County at the top of each excerpt above shows the number of undervotes. The number of undervotes in HD-12 was 18,385 but just 6,774 in HD-09. (These figures are calculated by subtracting from the total vote in each district the number of voters who voted in at least one race on the ballot but skipped the race in question.) The rate of undervoting was therefore 31.89% in HD-12 but just 10.63% in HD-09.


Specifically, 69 votes were cast in precinct 0106 CLEVELAND-01-B for the HD-09 race, while 330 were cast in the same precinct for the HD-12 race, for a total of 399 votes across both state House races. Therefore, we know that 17.29% (of the total of 399) votes were cast in HD-09 and 82.81% in HD-12.


However, because of the higher undervote in HD-12, we anticipate that we should allocate more than 82.81% of the presidential vote in precinct 0106 CLEVELAND-01-B to the sub-precinct in HD-12 (and conversely, less to the sub-precinct in HD-09). To determine how much more (or less) of the vote to allocate, we have developed four different methods:


* The Votes Cast method
* The Undervote Factor method
* The Registered Voters method
* The Census Population method


Each of these methods is described in detail below. While we consider all four methods accurate, we list them above in order of our preference, ranging from most to least accurate. When calculating statewide results by district, we use the most accurate method possible based on the data available, which may result in using a combination of methods for a given state.


Note that, because of changes in precinct maps, population totals, and turnout, we cannot use election results information from past elections to assign split precincts for any subsequent elections.


Note: Due to voting patterns within a precinct (particularly as a result of racial polarization and residential segregation patterns), estimates for split precincts using any of the above methods will have unknowable error bars.


1. Votes Cast ("VC") Method


The Votes Cast method is our first choice because we believe that it most accurately reflects actual voter behavior. This method relies on the total number of ballots that were cast in each portion of each sub-precinct. Note that this information is not provided by all counties, in which case the VC method cannot be used.


In the example shown above of Cuyahoga County's precinct 0106 CLEVELAND-01-B, a total of 69 votes were cast for the Democratic and Republican candidates in the 2016 race for HD-09. However, an additional 23 ballots were cast by voters registered in this sub-precinct who undervoted in this race (for instance, they may have voted in the presidential race but not the state House race). In the HD-12 portion of this precinct, a total of 330 votes were cast for the one candidate on the ballot (a Democrat), with 117 undervotes.


Two other types of votes can also affect the total vote: overvotes (where a voter impermissibly attempts to cast votes for more candidates than permitted) and write-ins. In the present example, there were no overvotes or write-ins in either state House race in precinct 0106 CLEVELAND-01-B. (While undervotes are common, overvotes and write-ins are uncommon in congressional and legislative races.)


Note: Figures describing the total vote are often identified by different names depending on jurisdiction. One common term is "cards counted." The composition of the total vote may also vary and may not include overvotes and/or write-ins.

2. Undervote Factor ("UF") Method


Many counties do not provide total vote figures that would allow us to use the VC method. If possible, we then turn to this method, which requires us to calculate what we call the Undervote Factor, or UF.


To calculate the UF, we compare the total number of votes cast in a congressional or legislative contest in a given county in every precinct that isn't split with the total number of votes cast in those same precincts for the statewide race we are analyzing. We then divide the former by the latter. This gives us the UF, expressed in percentage terms.


Note: We use all valid votes for all candidates plus write-ins, though we do not include write-ins in our final calculations for reasons discussed in our "Write-in votes" section above.


To illustrate by way of example, we offer hypothetical results from a county that is home to two state House districts, HD-01 and HD-02. These two districts share a certain number of split precincts and also are made up of a certain number of unsplit precincts. These results are shown in the table below.

3. Registered Voters ("RV") Method


Both the VC and UF methods rely on congressional or legislative races taking place simultaneously with statewide races in order to allocate the vote in split precincts. In certain elections, however, no congressional districts are on the ballot at the same time as certain statewide races.[16] In other elections, none or only some state legislative districts in a given chamber are on the ballot at the same time as certain statewide races.[17]


In such situations, we turn to the Registered Voters ("RV") method, which splits precincts based on how many voters are registered to vote in each sub-precinct. This method, which we’ve employed most often in Florida and Virginia, relies on the voter registration numbers produced closest to Election Day; some counties produce this data for the end of the month, while some produce it for the start of the new month. This method includes voters who are listed as active and have voted in recent elections and those listed as inactive who have not voted recently but remain eligible to vote for the time being.

4. Census Population ("CP") Method


In certain circumstances, when a new map has yet to be used, precincts are split in new ways for which election officials have yet to produce registered voter data for sub-precincts. In such cases, we resort to the Census Population method, which involves determining how many people live in each sub-precinct.


To do this, we match population data from the Census Bureau's decennial census to census block GIS shapefiles. We also match the census block shapefiles to precinct shapefiles.


The census population data comes from the Public Law (P.L.) 94-171 redistricting summary files, which are available at the census block level for entire states on the Census Bureau website. Options available include obtaining block-level population data for the total population or the voting age (18+) population ("VAP"). Prior to 2022, we used the total population. Starting in 2022, we have used the VAP because it more closely reflects the eligible voter population.


Census block shapefiles can be found via the Census Bureau's TIGER/line data sets grouped by state. Matching the two data sets requires aligning the block FIPS[18] code (also known as a Geographic Identifier, or GEOID), which is a unique 15-digit identifier. Using GIS software or spreadsheets, we join the block population to the block shapefile and create a new vector by using the GEOID as the join and target field. We then save the output as a new census block shapefile layer.


We then match the census block shapefiles to a precinct shapefile obtained from state or local election officials. We do this with a tool known as a spatial join, which matches two different shapefiles that have the same coordinate reference system by finding where and to what degree they overlap. This spatial join creates a new census block shapefile that adds a new data column containing the precinct that corresponds to each block. Alternatively, we use statewide files compiled by the Voting and Election Science Team (VEST), which has also joined precinct information to each census block. 


Note that precinct shapefile layers are rarely a perfect geometric match with census block shapefiles, since precinct layers are often simplified to reduce file size or created from sources that turned raster layers (flat images such as PDF files) into vector layers (coordinate-based geometry). Due to such mismatches, shapefile polygons that represent individual precincts may improperly align with a census block polygon or may align with the wrong block polygon. This method also presents problems when precincts are drawn in a way that splits census blocks, even if the two shapefile sources are a perfect geometric match. Additionally, census shapefiles may use different county lines than the precinct shapefiles, resulting in portions of precincts assigned to the wrong county. 


To error-check the spatially joined census block shapefile, we look for blocks that are mislabeled due to county boundary line changes or misalignment, and we visually compare it with the precinct shapefile using GIS software. When precincts split census blocks, we use aerial satellite imagery to visually examine whether there appear to be more residential buildings and residents in a block that corresponds to one precinct or another and assign the block to whichever precinct has the majority or plurality of the block. 


Starting in 2022, we have a new source of error to deal with: census differential privacy policy. To protect the privacy of individuals living in census blocks, the census essentially publishes incorrect data at the block level. This results in official census numbers showing sometimes a dozen people living in elementary schools, strip malls, Burger Kings, and in the middle of freeway interchanges. While people can and do live in places like these, they do not do so at anywhere near the frequency encountered in the census data. When encountering a precinct portion with a very small population (fewer than 20), we typically check Google streetview and/or satellite maps for any signs of habitation, and often, finding nothing, zero out the population as a result.


Once we have the number of residents by sub-precinct, we use the data to assign presidential votes. By way of example, in Chesterfield, Virginia's precinct 209 - SALEM CHURCH, a total of 4,864 people resided in the portion located in the version of House District 62 ("HD-62") that was used in the 2019 elections at the time of the 2010 U.S. Census, while another 1,124 resided in the portion located in the 2019 version of House District 66 ("HD-66").


To resolve the split in this precinct, we use the number of residents in each sub-precinct to calculate how much of each precinct is in each state House district, as shown below.
