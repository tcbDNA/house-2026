Decoding the Early Vote: What We Can, And Can’t, Learn From the Early Voting Window

VoteHub is launching our early vote tracker for the 2026 midterms, in partnership with L2. Here's how and why we will be interpreting the ballot return data up until November.

September 23, 2026

by

Zachary Donnini

Today, VoteHub launched its 2026 midterm early vote tracker, built in partnership with L2. The tracker follows mail ballot requests, absentee returns, and early in-person voting, while modeling the composition of the electorate by partisanship, race, 2024 vote, and estimated 2026 vote choice, with county-level aggregations and confidence intervals. VoteHub also collects some early vote data directly, but our partnership with L2 allows us to pair those returns with much richer voter-file modeling.

How do we figure out which voters are more likely to support a certain candidate? The most important indicator is party registration, which many states provide. Voters who are registered Democrats are more likely to vote for Democrats, while voters who are registered Republicans are more likely to vote for Republicans. In some states, voters are not registered with a party, but observing which party primaries they have participated in in the past can inform assumptions about which party they affiliate with. That is not quite as strong a signal, and it can be a mixed one for any individual voter, but it is still informative.

Many voters, however, are not registered with a major party or do not consistently participate in one party’s primary. In those cases, we can look at where a voter lives and model characteristics such as race. For example, an unaffiliated voter whom we model as Black and who lives in an overwhelmingly Harris-supporting precinct in southern Houston turning out to vote early in-person in the Texas U.S. Senate race would generally be a good sign for James Talarico, while a white voter from a strongly Trump-voting precinct in the Texas Panhandle would generally be a good sign for Ken Paxton. Either way, our uncertainty is much higher in a state without party registration, but it is still possible to draw meaningful conclusions about the types of voters who are turning out in states like Texas and Georgia.

Once we know which voters are turning out and which party they are more likely to support, there are two distinct but important reasons to track the early vote.

Understanding the Election Night Count

It is incredibly important for VoteHub to track and understand the early vote because it informs our coverage on election night. Election night is not only about trying to forecast what the final result will be, but also understanding how we are likely to get there over the course of election night and the following days.

We view our work on Election Day as an important part of making American democracy more understandable by providing detailed election data and clear, nonpartisan analysis. Vote-counting mirages can be difficult to cover, and it can be even harder to explain why a candidate who is leading the reported vote may not ultimately win the race. That makes it important for us to understand, as accurately as possible, which votes are still outstanding and what those votes are likely to look like before election night begins.

For instance, in the Michigan U.S. Senate race, we are closely tracking the composition of early in-person and absentee ballots at the precinct level, because the order in which different vote methods are reported can shape the trajectory of the results. We are also looking at which voters remain so we can model what the Election Day vote might look like if that becomes important to understanding the count. In Arizona U.S. House races, we are closely tracking the early mail vote so we have a better sense of what kinds of ballots are likely to remain after Election Day; Arizona officials note that early ballots received on Election Day are generally tabulated in the days afterward.

The same idea applies in Alaska, where early in-person, absentee, and questioned ballots are counted on a different and slower schedule than in-person Election Day ballots. Many absentee and late early in-person votes are also added a week or more after most other races will be called. The vote method landscape is still very much in flux from state to state and cycle to cycle, so much of this work is about estimating and tracking what we should expect from each type of ballot once the counting begins.

What the Early Vote Can Tell Us About the Outcome

The second reason is what many people are actually coming for: the early vote can help inform our view of which candidates are more likely to win. Republican and Democratic candidates will generally perform better if they can do two things: first, persuade more swing voters to support them; and second, enjoy a turnout advantage, with the voters who actually show up being more favorable to their party than the broader pool of registered voters. The more a candidate benefits from the second, the less they need to rely on the first.

This may be a particularly important early vote season. This cycle, Democrats may have an unusually favorable turnout advantage: they now have the higher-propensity coalition among the two major political parties, while also benefiting from some of the traditional advantages of being the out-party, including a potential enthusiasm gap and relatively depressed Republican turnout. This idea is supported by enthusiasm gaps in surveys and turnout patterns in the 2025 elections and subsequent special elections. A huge part of the public case that analysts are making for a strong Democratic year rests on the idea that the electorate that actually turns out in November could be more favorable to Democrats than registered voter generic-ballot averages suggest. So far this cycle, that adjustment has generally moved the numbers toward Democrats.

So, from a turnout perspective, we have set a fairly high bar for Democrats in our forecast. In many states, the early vote should give us some indication of whether they are meeting that bar, or perhaps even exceeding it. But we will have to be careful in how we interpret the data. Early vote analysis is difficult, and it is especially prone to overinterpretation.

First, it is important to understand that early vote analysis tells us who has voted, but it does not tell us how those voters actually voted. If we think James Talarico would be competitive in an electorate that looks like Trump +11, and the electorate instead begins to look more like Trump +8 because Democratic turnout is unusually strong, that would plainly be encouraging for Talarico. But turnout is only one part of the equation. If late-deciding voters shift sharply toward Ken Paxton in the final weeks, perhaps in response to campaign advertising or other developments, that could offset a turnout advantage.

Second, it is important to remember that many voters will still cast their ballots on Election Day, and that stronger early voting for a party than in a previous election can sometimes just mean they are cannibalizing voters who otherwise still would have voted on Election Day. Vote-method patterns remain very fluid from year to year, so it would be a mistake to assume that because a party’s early vote turnout is five points better than it was in 2024, its final turnout will also be five points better, or even certainly better at all. It is generally better for a party to bank as many votes as possible early, but the key is to acknowledge the uncertainty and look deeper into the composition of the electorate to understand who is actually turning out.

For example, an important component of our analysis involves stratifying the early vote by past turnout. Someone who voted in 2020, 2022 and 2024 is generally much more likely to vote again than someone who is registered but did not vote in any of those three elections. What is especially encouraging for a party is when it is not simply turning out its most reliable voters early who would most likely participate in the end, but also bringing out lower-propensity voters who might otherwise have been less certain to participate.

To illustrate why controlling turnout propensity matters, it is useful to look back at Arizona in 2024 and break down the state’s early mail returns on October 21st, which was 15 days before Election Day.

Through October 21, Democrats were running roughly even with Republicans in Arizona’s mail-ballot returns. But low-propensity Democratic turnout was extremely weak, while Democrats were moving through their pool of high-propensity voters more quickly than Republicans. This was hardly unexpected, as Arizona Republicans had been voting later in recent elections, whether by mail or in person, including the 2020 presidential election and the 2022 midterm election.

As Republican ballot returns picked up, the party caught up among voters who had participated in all three of the 2020, 2022 and 2024 elections, while building a much larger advantage among lower-propensity voters. Harris was actually leading in Arizona when roughly half of the vote had been reported, but Republicans still had far more high-propensity voters who had not yet cast or returned their ballots. Many of those votes arrived in the closing days of the election and on Election Day, and Trump ultimately won the state comfortably. Democrat Ruben Gallego defeated Republican Kari Lake in the U.S. Senate race on the same ballot, a good example of a situation where a persuasion edge is enough to overcome poor turnout dynamics. But the asymmetric turnout did still help Lake overperform polling that correctly identified her struggles with swing voters.

Our early vote model does this at a more granular level, estimating a turnout propensity for each voter based on a variety of factors. The model recognizes that high-propensity voters who have not yet voted early are still relatively likely to turn out on Election Day, while it is more concerning for a party if it is quickly exhausting its pool of reliable voters without also seeing elevated turnout among lower-propensity voters.

However, it is still important to be careful, even when controlling for past turnout. Good early vote analysis hinges on waiting until a meaningful number of ballots have been cast. In a state like North Carolina, for instance, we can track mail returns for months, but mail ballots accounted for only about five percent of the vote in 2024. We will still display that data, and there may be interesting things to learn from it, just as there may be from the first week of early in-person voting in Virginia. But voting patterns can change considerably over the course of the early voting period, so anything we learn before mid-October will probably be fairly limited. Caution, and a willingness to embrace uncertainty, should be paramount when interpreting and discussing those early developments.

Sometimes, even the full pre-Election Day vote does not tell us very much. In states where a large share of voters still cast ballots on Election Day, such as Iowa, Michigan, and Ohio, the pre-Election Day electorate may be both too small and too skewed toward particular voting methods to give us at all a clear picture of the final electorate. The pre-election vote we do get in the Midwest is also extremely Democratic simply because it is disproportionately made up of mail ballots. By contrast, states like North Carolina, Texas, Georgia and Florida can be much more informative because a much larger share of the electorate votes before Election Day, often through in-person early voting as well as by mail. In 2026, for example, we expect between 68 to 72 percent of ballots in these four states to be cast before Election Day. The more of the electorate we can observe, and the more representative those voters are of the electorate that ultimately turns out, the more we can reasonably hope to learn.
